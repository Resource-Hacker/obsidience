# 'My cancer spread while I waited months for surgery': The cost of England's cancer wait times

## Published: 3 October 2025 | Authors: Chloe Hayward, Hugh Pym | Source: BBC News

**Cancer care has got worse since the government announced plans to speed up waiting times and save more lives, according to BBC analysis.**

## The key facts

| Metric | Current | Target (March 2027) |
|--------|---------|---------------------|
| Patients starting treatment within 62 days | 69.9% | 80% |
| Target for 2029 | — | 85% |
| Additional treatment starts needed before 2029 | — | ~54,000 |
| Extra treatment starts per month to hit target | — | ~3,000 (≈15% increase) |

## The evidence

- Earlier this year, the government's **National Cancer Plan** and NHS Planning Guidance set the target that 80% of patients in England would start treatment within 62 days from urgent GP referral by March 2027.
- Despite this ambition, NHS England data shows performance has got **worse**, not better: only 69.9% of patients now start treatment within the 62-day timeframe, down from 71.9%.
- **Prof Pat Price**, a leading UK clinical academic oncologist, says the targets are now "simply impossible to hit" after what she describes as a "complete loss of momentum." She attributes the failures to a "complete lack of a plan or accountability" — noting that while a new independent cancer board was planned, it is still not known who will lead it.

## The human cost

**Alan, 70**, was diagnosed with lung cancer after a nodule was found in June last year. He was meant to be operated on within weeks but waited nearly five months, by which time the cancer had spread, making his surgery more complex. He was told the cancer had cleared awaiting his one-year review, but the operation has left him in debilitating pain.

"I used to play golf and get out and about. Now I can't do any of the things I love," says Alan.

For every month a cancer patient waits before starting treatment, their chance of survival falls by around **10%**. Prof Price says thousands of patients "are going to die unnecessarily, just because the government can't get their act together to treat people on time."

## Why the gap is widening

Trusts with the sharpest declines in performance — including Buckinghamshire Healthcare, University Hospitals Dorset, Milton Keynes University Hospital, East Cheshire, and Torbay and South Devon — told the BBC they face pressures across the board. **Rising demand, workforce shortages and diagnostic bottlenecks** have all played a part, alongside industrial action, staff sickness, reliance on outside diagnostic providers, and major changes to IT systems.

## Progress in some areas

Some trusts are gaining traction. **Salisbury District Hospital** has made visible progress in skin and prostate cancer through "joined-up care" — for example, an innovative procedure that checks tumour margins intra-operatively, eliminating the need for follow-up operations. Consultant plastic surgeon Neil Wickham performed such surgery on patient Diane, who now has hardly a trace of a large cancerous lesion that was removed from her cheek.

But even Salisbury is not yet hitting targets. Staff say there is no single fix — just a relentless focus on getting the basics right: ensuring patients know about appointments and turn up, keeping IT systems working, recruiting the right people, filling surgical lists, and expanding capacity.
