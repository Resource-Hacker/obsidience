**Lead:** Twenty-five years on from the 2001 attacks, the BBC's security correspondent writes that 9/11 has come to define a generation of foreign, security and intelligence policy — yet the lessons remain only partially learnt.

**Key points:**

- **Intelligence failures persist:** The 9/11 Commission Report cited "failures of imagination, policy, capabilities and management" in US intelligence. While sharing between Western agencies has improved since 2001, major failures of assessment and strategic planning have continued.

- **Post-9/11 failures:** The 2003 Iraq War was based on deeply flawed intelligence about WMDs, leading to MI6's worst reputational setback since the 1950s. The 2005 7/7 London bombings and 2017 Manchester Arena bombing were also intelligence lapses. The 2022 Russian invasion of Ukraine and 2023 Hamas raid on Israel are other cited examples.

- **Changed threat landscape:** The terrorist threat has evolved. Lord Jonathan Evans (former head of MI5) says "the picture has changed" — "Non-state terrorism remains a serious concern but state threats… have become increasingly serious and are at least as severe a national security concern as non-state terrorism. These threats come, in different forms, from Russia, Iran, their proxies and from China."

- **Strategic miscalculation:** Sir Alex Younger (former head of MI6) warned the West "missed the rise of China as a military power and as a strategic challenge." The West assumed China's economic integration would lead to political convergence; instead it overlooked China's strategic investments in semiconductors, critical minerals, batteries, ports and telecommunications.

- **Moral costs:** The post-9/11 period saw widespread human rights abuses — the CIA's "rendition" program, Guantanamo Bay detention without trial, and 2025 deportations to El Salvador's Terrorism Confinement Centre. The UK was also implicated: MI6's 2004 abduction and rendition of Abdelhakim Belhaj to Libya, resulting in his torture by Gaddafi's regime. The case led to a 2018 public apology.

- **The core problem remains:** As Sam Olsen of the security intelligence firm Sibylline put it, the issue is often "less a failure of intelligence collection than a failure of strategic interpretation." Having pieces of the jigsaw is not the same as seeing the picture.

**Uncertainties and qualifications:** This is one reporter's assessment published as part of the BBC's *InDepth* series. It presents several senior officials' recollections and analysis rather than a comprehensive audit of intelligence successes. Some events — like whether Trump "lacked a counter-strategy" in response to Iran's 2024 Strait of Hormuz closure — are presented as arguable judgments rather than settled conclusions. The report itself notes that "other noteworthy instances" of intelligence failure should be considered, and that questions about joining the Iraq and Afghanistan wars "continue to reverberate" — indicating no single settled answer exists.
