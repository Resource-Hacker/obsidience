## The move
Mayors in England will be given the power to impose an uncapped "tourist tax" on overnight stays in hotels, bed and breakfasts and other accommodation. The government says the extra revenue could be invested in local areas.

Under the plans, levies will be set as a percentage of accommodation costs rather than a flat fee. Local leaders will have until early 2028 to clarify how raised revenue should be reinvested and may offer exemptions (e.g. for campsites) but not entire localities.

## Hospitality reaction
UKHospitality chief executive Allen Simpson warned it would add **£100–£120 on average to a family holiday**, putting jobs at risk:

> "If you only devolve one tax raising power, of course local mayors are going to pull that lever until it snaps."

He noted that low-cost accommodation might not open in shoulder seasons and families would have "that little bit less money in their pocket."

Conservative shadow housing secretary David Simmonds argued VAT is already higher than in other countries, adding the tourism tax "on top." Reform UK leader Nigel Farage called it a "holiday tax" his party's mayors "won't touch."

## Government defence
Local Government Secretary Angela Rayner said the measure would give mayors control to "reinvest funding where it's needed most," supporting "local services, public spaces and attractions that both residents and visitors rely on."

## Background
The idea was first raised under PM Sir Keir Starmer in November 2026. Similar schemes exist in Scotland (a capped 5% levy in Edinburgh) and across Europe (New York, Amsterdam, Rome). Two English cities already run voluntary schemes: Manchester (£1/room/night) and Liverpool (£2/night).

Labour mayors in Liverpool, West Yorkshire, London, the West of England, South Yorkshire, West Midlands and York & North Yorkshire welcomed the announcement. Reform and Conservative mayors are likely to oppose it. Mayor of London Lord Khan reportedly supports a maximum 5% cap. Mayor Ben Houchen (Tees Valley) had previously ruled it out. A bill will be introduced to Parliament "in due course."
