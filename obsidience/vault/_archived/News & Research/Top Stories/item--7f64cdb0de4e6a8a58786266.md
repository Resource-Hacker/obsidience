## August was joint-hottest month on record

**Source:** BBC News — *Reported* 10 Sep 2026; *Published* 13 Nov 2024  
**Cited evidence:** `source://5b42d36d-a819-4d14-a5c9-59152b20691a` (RSS feed entry), `source://c8b5a3a0-27d1-4518-a58a-536323bff3df` (full article)

### Record heat
The EU's Copernicus Climate Change Service said August 2026 was the **joint-hottest month on record**, tying July 2023. Average temperatures were **1.65°C above pre-industrial levels**, surpassing the 1.5°C limit set by international climate agreements.

> "A single month above 1.5°C does not mean it has been permanently crossed, but it shows a trend towards breaching that limit."

### Drivers: climate change and El Niño
Researchers attributed the record to a combination of **climate change** and a **strengthening El Niño** — a natural Pacific climate pattern that releases stored ocean heat into the atmosphere and nudges global temperatures upward for around a year. The current El Niño is still strengthening and could become one of the most powerful on record, with its biggest temperature impacts expected over the coming year.

### European heat
The month capped **western Europe's warmest summer on record**, exceeding the deadly 2003 heatwave. In the UK, it was the hottest summer since records began in 1884 — the Met Office found this mark was made around **130 times more likely** by human-caused climate change.

### Impacts and costs
The summer's heat disrupted schools, strained hospitals, hit food production, fuelled fires and drought, and claimed "tens of thousands of lives" (Dr Clair Barnes, Imperial College London).

> "Until we stop burning fossil fuels these records will continue to tumble, and the extremes will worsen faster than we are able to adapt. Crucially, how much worse we allow it to get remains within our hands."

### Expert commentary
- Dr Samantha Burgess, Copernicus strategic lead for climate: described August as "a remarkable month in the global climate record" and noted impacts "increasingly being felt by communities, economies and ecosystems across the world."
- UN climate chief Simon Stiell: called the evidence "irrefutable" and framed the records as "the spiralling price of humanity's fossil fuel addiction, and the global climate crisis it is fuelling."
